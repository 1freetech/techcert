# OSHistory.002: Commodity Money — Why Some Goods Work Better as Money

Commodity money is a physical good that circulates as money while also possessing nonmonetary usefulness or desirability.

## Learning objectives

After this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- define commodity money;
- explain the monetary importance of durability, portability, divisibility, recognizability, scarcity, acceptability, and relative stability;
- compare the strengths and weaknesses of cattle, grain, salt, cowries, silver, and gold as monetary goods;
- explain why quality verification matters in commodity exchange;
- explain how scales, weights, metallurgy, storage, transport, recordkeeping, and authentication supported commodity-money systems;
- explain why weighed metals created transaction and verification costs;
- explain how standardized coinage reduced some of those costs;
- explain why monetary history cannot be reduced to one universal barter-to-commodity-to-coin sequence.

## Historical framework

Commodity money should be treated as one important family of monetary arrangements. Historical economies could use physical commodities, credit, accounting ledgers, obligations, coins, and other payment technologies at the same time.

## Core properties

A strong monetary commodity tends to reduce the costs of:

- storage;
- transport;
- division;
- recognition and authentication;
- quality verification;
- supply uncertainty;
- finding counterparties willing to accept it.

## Bridge to coinage

When metal circulates by weight, each transaction can require weighing and purity verification. Standardized coinage can reduce those repeated verification costs by attaching recognizable marks, expected weights, denominations, or authoritative guarantees to metal units.

## Key takeaway

Commodity money is a selection problem. Goods become stronger monetary media when their physical properties, supply characteristics, and social acceptance reduce the friction of exchange.
