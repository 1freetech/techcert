Direct burial in fiber optics refers to the installation of specially designed fiber optic cables directly into the ground without the need for protective conduits or ducts. These cables are engineered to withstand harsh underground conditions for long-term, maintenance-free operation.

Direct burial fiber optic cables are constructed with multiple protective layers to endure soil pressure, moisture, temperature fluctuations, and even rodent interference.

At their core, they contain single-mode or multimode optical fibers housed in loose buffer tubes, which allow for thermal expansion and reduce microbending losses.

Surrounding these tubes are water-blocking materials—either gel-filled or water-swellable tape—that prevent moisture ingress and preserve signal integrity.

To reinforce the cable against mechanical stress, strength members such as aramid yarns (e.g., Kevlar) or fiber-reinforced plastic (FRP) rods are embedded within the structure.

In more rugged environments, an armored layer—typically corrugated steel tape or steel wire—is added to protect against crushing forces and rodent damage.

The outermost sheath is usually made of high-density polyethylene (HDPE), chosen for its resistance to abrasion, chemicals, and UV exposure.

Direct burial cables are typically laid using trenching or plowing methods, and they must be buried at regulated depths to avoid accidental excavation damage.

Warning tape or surface markers are often placed above the cable route to signal its presence.

These cables are ideal for long-distance outdoor networks, campus interconnects, and industrial sites where conduit installation is impractical or cost-prohibitive.

There are several variants of direct burial fiber cables, including armored, non-armored, loose tube gel-filled, and all-dielectric self-supporting (ADSS) types, each tailored to specific environmental and mechanical requirements.
