<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Today’s foundation is the relationship between <strong>voltage, c<a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2025/11/28/electrical-engineering-ac-vs-dc-current/">urrent</a>, <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/01/20/resistance-vs-resistivity/">resistance</a>, and power</strong>. These four quantities show up constantly when troubleshooting power supplies, motors, control circuits, <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/08/17/semiconductor-training-product-burn-in-testing-and-electronic-device-reliability/">semiconductor equipment</a>, and data-center hardware.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/01/23/electrical-technician-training-current-vs-voltage/"><strong>Voltage (V)</strong></a> is electrical potential difference. Think of voltage as the electrical force available to move charge between two points. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- /wp:embed -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A standard U.S. receptacle should measure approximately <strong>120 V AC from hot to neutral</strong> and approximately <strong>120 V AC from hot to ground</strong>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Current (I)</strong> is the rate of electric charge flow, measured in <strong>amperes (A)</strong>. Equipment determines how much current flows based largely on its impedance and the applied voltage. A 120 V circuit does not automatically push the circuit breaker’s full rated current through every connected device.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:embed {"url":"https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XGyrjxPaIm4\u0026amp;pp=ygULQ3VycmVudCAoSSk%3D","type":"video","providerNameSlug":"youtube","responsive":true,"className":"wp-embed-aspect-16-9 wp-has-aspect-ratio"} -->

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XGyrjxPaIm4&amp;pp=ygULQ3VycmVudCAoSSk%3D
</div></figure>
<!-- /wp:embed -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Resistance (R)</strong> is opposition to current flow, measured in <strong>ohms (Ω)</strong>. For a simple resistive DC circuit, Ohm’s law is:<math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>V</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>I</mi><mi>R</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">V = I R</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Rearranging gives:<math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>I</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mi>V</mi><mi>R</mi></mfrac><mspace width="2em"></mspace><mi>R</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mi>V</mi><mi>I</mi></mfrac></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">I=\frac{V}{R} \qquad R=\frac{V}{I}</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Power (P)</strong> is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or consumed, measured in <strong>watts (W)</strong>. For a basic DC or purely resistive circuit:<math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>V</mi><mi>I</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P=VI</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>So a 120 V resistive load drawing 5 A consumes approximately:<math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mn>120</mn><mo>×</mo><mn>5</mn><mo>=</mo><menclose notation="box"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mn>600</mn><mtext>&nbsp;W</mtext></mrow></mstyle></mstyle></mstyle></menclose></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">120\times5=\boxed{600\text{ W}}</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Field scenario</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You are troubleshooting a piece of equipment connected to a 120 V receptacle. Your multimeter shows:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Hot → Neutral: 120 V</strong><br><strong>Hot → Ground: 120 V</strong><br><strong>Neutral → Ground: 0.4 V</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:embed {"url":"https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P-W42tk-fWc\u0026amp;pp=ygUcIDEyMCBWIEFDIGZyb20gaG90IHRvIGdyb3VuZA%3D%3D","type":"video","providerNameSlug":"youtube","responsive":true,"className":"wp-embed-aspect-16-9 wp-has-aspect-ratio"} -->
<figure class="wp-block-embed is-type-video is-provider-youtube wp-block-embed-youtube wp-embed-aspect-16-9 wp-has-aspect-ratio"><div class="wp-block-embed__wrapper">
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P-W42tk-fWc&amp;pp=ygUcIDEyMCBWIEFDIGZyb20gaG90IHRvIGdyb3VuZA%3D%3D
</div></figure>
<!-- /wp:embed -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Those readings are generally consistent with a properly energized receptacle. Neutral and ground should normally have very little voltage between them at the receptacle, although a small voltage can appear because current flowing through the neutral conductor creates some voltage drop.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Now suppose you measure:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Hot → Neutral: 72 V</strong><br><strong>Hot → Ground: 120 V</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That combination immediately makes the <strong>neutral path suspicious</strong>. Hot-to-ground indicates the hot conductor has approximately the expected voltage, while hot-to-neutral is abnormally low. A loose, damaged, or high-resistance neutral connection becomes one possible troubleshooting target.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">New term: Voltage drop</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Voltage drop</strong> is the reduction in voltage across a conductor or component when current flows through resistance or impedance.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:embed {"url":"https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FUErtfqDxeE\u0026amp;pp=ygUMVm9sdGFnZSBkcm9w0gcJCRsMAYcqIYzv","type":"video","providerNameSlug":"youtube","responsive":true,"className":"wp-embed-aspect-16-9 wp-has-aspect-ratio"} -->
<figure class="wp-block-embed is-type-video is-provider-youtube wp-block-embed-youtube wp-embed-aspect-16-9 wp-has-aspect-ratio"><div class="wp-block-embed__wrapper">
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FUErtfqDxeE&amp;pp=ygUMVm9sdGFnZSBkcm9w0gcJCRsMAYcqIYzv
</div></figure>
<!-- /wp:embed -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Imagine a cable feeding a machine. The source measures <strong>120 V</strong>, but only <strong>110 V</strong> reaches the machine while it is operating. The missing 10 V is being dropped somewhere along the circuit.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A useful troubleshooting principle is:<math display="block"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>V</mi><mrow><mi>d</mi><mi>r</mi><mi>o</mi><mi>p</mi></mrow></msub><mo>=</mo><mi>I</mi><mo>×</mo><mi>R</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">V_{drop}=I\times R</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Higher current or higher resistance produces greater voltage drop.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A loose connector is therefore dangerous because its resistance can increase. Current flowing through that resistance produces heat:<math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><msup><mi>I</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mi>R</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P=I^2R</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>For example, if a bad connection develops <strong>0.5 Ω</strong> resistance while carrying <strong>10 A</strong>:<math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>10</mn><msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>0.5</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mo>=</mo><menclose notation="box"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mn>50</mn><mtext>&nbsp;W</mtext></mrow></mstyle></mstyle></mstyle></menclose></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P=(10)^2(0.5)=\boxed{50\text{ W}}</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That tiny connection could be dissipating <strong>50 watts as heat</strong>, even though the rest of the equipment appears operational.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Practice</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>1. A 24 V DC control circuit has a 12 Ω load. How much current flows?</strong><math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>I</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mn>24</mn><mn>12</mn></mfrac><mo>=</mo><menclose notation="box"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mn>2</mn><mtext>&nbsp;A</mtext></mrow></mstyle></mstyle></mstyle></menclose></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">I=\frac{24}{12}=\boxed{2\text{ A}}</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The circuit draws 2 amperes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>2. A device operates at 120 V and draws 8 A. Approximately how much power does it consume?</strong><math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>120</mn><mo>×</mo><mn>8</mn><mo>=</mo><menclose notation="box"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mn>960</mn><mtext>&nbsp;W</mtext></mrow></mstyle></mstyle></mstyle></menclose></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P=120\times8=\boxed{960\text{ W}}</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>3. You measure 120 V hot-to-ground but only 65 V hot-to-neutral. Which conductor deserves investigation first?</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Answer: Neutral.</strong> Hot-to-ground provides evidence that the hot conductor is supplying approximately the expected voltage. The abnormal hot-to-neutral measurement makes the neutral connection a logical troubleshooting target.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>4. Why should resistance or continuity normally be measured on a de-energized circuit?</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Answer:</strong> A multimeter supplies its own small test signal when measuring resistance or continuity. External circuit voltage can invalidate the measurement and potentially damage the meter or create a safety hazard.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>5. Challenge: A connector has 0.2 Ω of unwanted resistance and carries 15 A. How much heat is generated at the connection?</strong><math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><msup><mi>I</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mi>R</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P=I^2R</annotation></semantics></math><math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>15</mn><msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>0.2</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P=(15)^2(0.2)</annotation></semantics></math><math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>225</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>0.2</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo><mo>=</mo><menclose notation="box"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="false"><mstyle scriptlevel="0" displaystyle="true"><mrow><mn>45</mn><mtext>&nbsp;W</mtext></mrow></mstyle></mstyle></mstyle></menclose></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P=225(0.2)=\boxed{45\text{ W}}</annotation></semantics></math></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That result explains why seemingly small amounts of unwanted resistance can create serious heating at high-current terminals.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"fontSize":"medium"} -->
<p class="has-medium-font-size"><strong>Voltage → electrical potential difference</strong><br><strong>Current → flow of electric charge</strong><br><strong>Resistance → opposition to current</strong><br><strong>Power → rate of energy transfer</strong><br><strong>Voltage drop → voltage lost across resistance/impedance</strong><br><strong>Open circuit → broken current path</strong><br><strong>Short circuit → unintended low-impedance path</strong><br><strong>Load → component consuming electrical power</strong></p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Field rule to remember:</strong> <strong>Voltage tells you what electrical potential is present. Current tells you what is flowing. Resistance helps explain why that amount flows. Power tells you how quickly electrical energy is being transferred.</strong></p>
