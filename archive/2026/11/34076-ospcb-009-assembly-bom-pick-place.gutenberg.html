OSPCB.009 follows a circuit board beyond fabrication into PCB assembly (PCBA). A factory can manufacture a bare board correctly and still populate the wrong component, rotate a connector, or omit a part. Assembly therefore needs a matched bill of materials (BOM), component placement coordinates, solder-paste information, and a clearly identified revision. The previous OSPCB.008 fabrication handoff lesson covered Gerbers and drill data. Here, the goal is to make the populated board match the electrical design.

1. A BOM identifies the actual parts

A bill of materials ties designators such as R1 or U1 to part quantities, values, packages and preferably exact manufacturer part numbers. A generic description like “10 kΩ resistor” can leave uncertainty about tolerance, power rating, case size and temperature rating. Record approved alternatives explicitly instead of relying on an assembler to infer them. KiCad 10's schematic BOM exporter can generate configurable outputs; the official Schematic Editor manual explains the exporter. Verify sourcing availability and the purchasing version independently.

A do-not-populate (DNP) item requires special attention. An intentionally unassembled footprint may remain on the board while its component should not be supplied or placed. Compare BOM inclusion settings with the assembler's accepted convention and final review screen. OSPCB.008 on vias and manufacturing checks explains why the bare board can remain valid without every possible part installed.

2. Placement data tell machines where to put parts

A component placement list, sometimes called a centroid or pick-and-place file, contains designators, positions, orientations and board side. In KiCad 10, use File → Fabrication Outputs → Component Placement in PCB Editor. The KiCad PCB Editor reference documents coordinate-origin, unit and footprint-exclusion options. Match the exported origin and rotation conventions to the assembly provider's requirements. An electrically correct footprint can still be physically rotated incorrectly during production.

Review top and bottom placements separately where appropriate. Check polarized capacitors, diodes, pin-one indicators, connectors and IC orientation against the schematic, silkscreen and assembly preview. Never assume a 0° rotation in one supplier's software means exactly the same physical orientation in another.

3. Solder paste is not solder mask

The F.Paste and B.Paste layers specify solder-paste apertures for surface-mount assembly. Solder-mask layers serve a different purpose: they expose or cover copper during board manufacture. A stencil uses paste aperture data to deposit material on designated pads before components are placed and reflowed. Incorrect apertures can increase bridging, tombstoning or insufficient-solder risks. Follow the assembler's stencil rules rather than applying generic reductions without engineering approval. JLCPCB's stencil guidance explains how selected assembly parts affect stencil preparation.

A common assembly sequence is paste printing, component placement, reflow, inspection and any required through-hole operations. The manufacturer's PCBA process description explains the stages. OSPCB.007 on signal integrity provides context for why part placement and routing interact with board performance.

4. A practical assembly handoff review

Before ordering, compare every populated designator across the schematic, BOM and placement file. Confirm component package, manufacturer number, board side, rotation, reference origin, DNP status and matching revision. Inspect the paste outputs, board outline, assembly notes, approved substitutions and the vendor's preview. The JLCPCB assembly checklist and PCBWay's KiCad file guide describe typical handoff fields.

Practice: A fictional assembly job lists U1 in the BOM but not the position file; D1 appears with a 180° rotation; R9 is marked DNP. Explain which discrepancies require clarification before release. Do not treat the rotation value alone as a proven defect; inspect the supplier preview and part orientation. The task is document review, not unapproved operation of reflow or placement equipment.

A finished assembly order should preserve approved outputs and revision history. OSPCB.006 on decoupling placement shows why a correct component installed in the wrong place can undermine an otherwise valid circuit.

Takeaway: Gerbers define the bare PCB. The BOM defines parts. Placement data define positions. Paste layers support solder deposition. Cross-checking all four turns a board design into a reproducible assembly package.
