<p>A <strong>MAC address (Media Access Control address)</strong> is a globally unique identifier assigned to a network interface controller (NIC) by the manufacturer. It operates at <strong>OSI Layer 2 (Data Link Layer)</strong> and is typically written in <strong>hexadecimal format</strong>, such as <code>00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E</code>. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"className":""} -->
<p>The MAC address consists of two parts: the <strong>Organizationally Unique Identifier (OUI)</strong>, which identifies the vendor, and the <strong>device identifier</strong>, which is specific to the NIC. MAC addresses are essential in <strong>local area network (LAN)</strong> communication, as they are used by switches to direct traffic and build MAC address tables for efficient data forwarding.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"className":""} -->
<p>MAC addresses are <strong>static</strong>, but they can be <strong>spoofed</strong> for privacy or testing purposes. Devices rely on <strong>ARP (Address Resolution Protocol)</strong> to map IP addresses to MAC addresses for proper delivery on IPv4 networks. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph {"className":""} -->
<p>In modern networking, MAC filtering can be used for <strong>access control</strong>, while technologies like <strong>Wake-on-LAN</strong> depend on the MAC address to send “magic packets” to specific devices. Understanding how MAC addresses influence <strong>packet delivery</strong>, <strong>network security</strong>, and <strong>device tracking</strong> is vital for configuring secure and efficient networking environments.</p>
