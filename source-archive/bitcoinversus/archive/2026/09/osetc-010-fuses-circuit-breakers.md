## Complete published lesson

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>OSETC is the Open-Source Electrical Technician Certification. In <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/30/osetc-009-open-circuits-short-circuits/">OSETC.009</a>, we learned how a short creates an unintended current path. Now we will learn how fuses and circuit breakers interrupt excessive current.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">1. What overcurrent means</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Overcurrent means current exceeds an equipment rating or a conductor’s allowable current. An overload happens along the normal current path, such as too many loads on a circuit. A short circuit creates an unintended low-resistance path. Both can require protective action.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">2. What a fuse does</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A conventional fuse contains a metal element designed to melt and open the circuit during specified overcurrent conditions. Once it operates, it must be replaced with the approved replacement after the cause is resolved. <a href="https://www.eaton.com/us/en-us/products/electronic-components/topics/fuse-technology.html">Eaton’s fuse reference</a> explains the element and its operating characteristics.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A fuse is not simply a piece of wire. Its rating and construction determine how it handles current, voltage, and fault energy. Never bypass it with wire, foil, or another improvised conductor.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">3. What a circuit breaker does</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A circuit breaker opens contacts when its protective mechanism operates. Depending on its design, it can respond to sustained overloads and short-circuit currents. It may be reset after the fault is resolved and the equipment is assessed as safe. <a href="https://www.eaton.com/us/en-us/products/electrical-circuit-protection/circuit-breakers/circuit-breakers-fundamentals.html">Eaton’s breaker guide</a> describes these functions.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">4. Three ratings to recognize</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>Current rating, in amperes:</strong> the device’s rated current under specified conditions. A 5 A fuse is not a promise that it opens instantly at 5.1 A; operating time depends on its time-current curve.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>Voltage rating:</strong> the permitted circuit voltage for its specified application. Check AC or DC suitability.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>Interrupting rating:</strong> the fault current the device can safely interrupt at the stated voltage. This is different from its normal current rating.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>These are only part of selection. Fuse class, speed, physical format, equipment compatibility, and manufacturer requirements also matter. A larger current rating is not a repair for repeated operation.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">5. Simple technician exercise</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Use a photograph, datasheet, or instructor-provided disconnected training device. Record its part number, current rating, voltage rating, and AC/DC designation. Find its interrupting rating in the approved datasheet. Do not remove installed devices or open an energized panel for this exercise.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Example: a label says 5 A and 250 V AC. Those markings identify two ratings, but they do not establish DC suitability, operating speed, or the correct replacement. Use the full part number and equipment specification.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">6. What to do when protection operates</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Record the equipment, affected loads, and circumstances. Follow the site’s isolation and troubleshooting procedure. Investigate the cause before replacement or reset. Do not repeatedly reset a tripping breaker or increase a fuse rating to keep equipment running.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Standard overcurrent protection does not guarantee protection from electric shock. An open protective device also does not prove all equipment conductors are de-energized; other sources may exist.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Quick review</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>Does a conventional blown fuse reset?</strong> No. <strong>Is interrupting rating the same as current rating?</strong> No. <strong>Should a larger fuse fix repeated failures?</strong> No; investigate the cause and follow the equipment specification.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Technician takeaway</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Fuses and breakers interrupt overcurrent according to their ratings and operating characteristics. Identify the device, understand its labels, and resolve the cause before restoring service.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
