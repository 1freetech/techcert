Liquid cooling is an advanced thermal management solution used in desktops to dissipate heat more effectively than traditional air cooling. It involves circulating a coolant—usually distilled water or a specialized liquid—through tubes connected to a water block mounted on the CPU or GPU.

Heat is transferred from the component to the coolant, then pushed through a radiator where fans remove the heat from the system.

There are two main types of liquid cooling systems: closed-loop (AIO – All-in-One) and custom-loop systems. AIO coolers are pre-assembled, maintenance-free units ideal for most users.

Custom loops offer higher performance and better aesthetics but require technical expertise to assemble, maintain, and leak-test.

A+ technicians should recognize the components involved: water block, pump, reservoir, tubing, and radiator. When installing liquid cooling, proper mounting pressure, thermal paste application, tubing clearance, and fan orientation are crucial.

They should also understand how BIOS or software utilities can control pump and fan speeds to fine-tune system thermals.

While liquid cooling offers quieter operation and improved temperature regulation—especially under heavy workloads like gaming or rendering—it introduces risk. Leaks, pump failures, or evaporation can compromise system stability or damage components.

Techs should inspect fittings regularly and understand how to diagnose issues like trapped air or poor contact.
