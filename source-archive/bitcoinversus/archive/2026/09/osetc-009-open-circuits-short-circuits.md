## Complete published lesson

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>OSETC is the Open-Source Electrical Technician Certification. After <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/09/30/osetc-008-voltage-drop-loose-connections/">OSETC.008: Voltage Drop and Loose Connections</a>, we will learn two common circuit conditions: a broken path and an unintended shortcut.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">1. Open means the path is interrupted</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>An open circuit has a gap that prevents current from flowing through that path. An open switch is a normal example. A broken wire or blown fuse can create an unwanted open. An open branch does not necessarily stop current in other parallel branches.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>An open circuit can still have voltage across the gap. A lamp being off does not prove the circuit is safe to touch.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">2. Short means a low-resistance shortcut</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A short circuit is a low-resistance connection between points that should not be directly connected. It can bypass a load or connect supply conductors together. Fault current depends on the source and total path resistance; it can be high enough to damage wiring or operate protective devices.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>For example, a broken lamp wire is an open. An unintended wire connecting around the lamp is a short across that lamp. <a href="https://www.ece.tufts.edu/en/1EK/textbook/circuits_openshort.html">Tufts University’s circuit reference</a> explains these two conditions.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">3. What a continuity test tells you</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Continuity checks whether resistance between the probes is below the meter’s beep threshold. A beep across an isolated wire is usually expected. A beep between points that should be isolated needs investigation. A beep alone does not prove a short: switches, windings, and parallel paths can also have low resistance.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>OL generally means the reading is beyond the selected measurement range. In continuity testing, it can indicate an open path or resistance above the meter’s threshold. Confirm probe contact and meter operation before interpreting the result.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Follow <a href="https://www.fluke.com/en-us/learn/blog/clamps/how-to-measure-signals-using-test-probes">Fluke’s measurement guidance</a>: remove circuit power before resistance or continuity testing. Stored energy must also be discharged by the approved procedure.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">4. A simple technician exercise</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Use a disconnected, isolated training switch with no power source attached. Put the black lead in COM and the red lead in the meter’s resistance/voltage input. Select continuity mode and touch the probes together to check the meter.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Place a probe on each switch terminal. With the switch open, expect no beep. Close the switch and expect a low-resistance reading or beep. Record both results. This checks only the isolated switch; it does not verify absence of voltage in an installed circuit.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Learn short circuits from the diagram or a circuit simulator. Do not deliberately short a battery or power supply. Before investigating real equipment, follow its isolation procedure and verify absence of voltage using suitable equipment.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">5. Quick review</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>A broken wire:</strong> an open path. <strong>An unintended connection around a load:</strong> a short across the load. <strong>A continuity beep:</strong> low resistance between the probes, interpreted against the circuit’s expected connections.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Practice question: an isolated switch beeps when closed and stops beeping when opened. Is that expected? <strong>Yes.</strong> The switch completes or interrupts the path.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Technician takeaway</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Trace the intended path first. An open interrupts it; a short creates a low-resistance shortcut. Compare measurements with the schematic before naming the fault.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
