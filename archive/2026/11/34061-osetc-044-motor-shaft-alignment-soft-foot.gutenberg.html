OSETC.044 explains why a motor and a coupled pump can vibrate, overheat or wear a coupling despite both machines continuing to run. Shaft alignment compares the rotational axes, while soft foot describes inconsistent contact between machine feet and their mounting surface. The lesson follows OSETC.043 on bearing condition. Readers will interpret recorded evidence and prepare a safe maintenance report, not perform adjustments on live equipment.

Two types of shaft misalignment

With parallel offset, two shafts point in roughly the same direction but their axes do not coincide. With angular misalignment, their axial directions differ. A motor-pump assembly can exhibit both conditions, in horizontal and vertical planes. A flexible coupling tolerates only the limits specified by its manufacturer. PRUFTECHNIK identifies misalignment mechanisms and consequences in its technical discussion of shaft faults.

Vibration alone does not prove misalignment. Imbalance, damaged bearings, structural looseness, electrical supply problems and resonance can create overlapping symptoms. Compare OSETC.040 on voltage imbalance and OSETC.039 on current imbalance before assigning a cause.

Why soft foot must be investigated first

Soft foot occurs when a motor's mounting foot does not rest consistently on the base. Tightening bolts against uneven support may distort the frame and shift shaft geometry. SKF's soft-foot documentation describes instrument-supported checks, while its TKSA 41 specifications identify alignment and soft-foot functionality. An approved inspection records readings for individual feet rather than relying on a single average.

Consider fictional movement readings of 0.02, 0.11, 0.03 and 0.04 millimeters at the four feet. The second location stands out and warrants investigation. Nevertheless, those numbers alone establish neither a failed installation nor a correction amount. Foot geometry, approved instrument procedures, applicable manufacturer tolerances and instrument uncertainty all matter. No universal adjustment threshold is implied.

Temperature can move a perfectly aligned cold machine

A machine that looks aligned at ambient temperature can change geometry after heat-up. Motor and pump housings may have different heights, expansion coefficients and operating temperatures. PRUFTECHNIK explains thermal growth and shaft movement. Installation targets should come from approved equipment drawings, a qualified alignment plan and the relevant coupling specification, not a generic number copied from another machine.

Laser shaft-alignment equipment measures axes and calculates geometry based on sensor readings and machine dimensions. Operators may record vertical and horizontal offset, angular deviation, instrument quality and indicated foot movements. Successful measurement does not eliminate the need to evaluate base integrity, pipe strain, coupling wear or thermal movement. The final verification should preserve the original and resulting reports for engineering review.

Safe inspection without manipulating machinery

Begin a simulated work order by documenting equipment identifiers, operating state, symptom history, last alignment report, vibration trends, foot condition and coupling details. List competing hypotheses and any missing evidence. Recording available information does not require opening a coupling guard. Actual measurements, machine rotation, foot-bolt adjustments and coupling work are activities for authorized, trained personnel under site procedures.

The OSHA hazardous-energy standard governs energy control during covered maintenance. Stopping a motor through its drive, including Safe Torque Off, is not automatically electrical isolation; see OSEEC.029 on STO. The site's LOTO lesson explains why a machine switched off may still contain hazardous energy.

Practice: distinguish facts from hypotheses

A hypothetical motor-pump unit shows a rising vibration trend following recent baseplate work. A soft-foot inspection is not yet available, and no alignment report accompanies the maintenance record. Write three plausible explanations, identify the evidence needed to distinguish them, and recommend a qualified inspection. Completion means preserving an authorized inspection report, evaluating corrective work against approved tolerances, and comparing post-maintenance conditions under equivalent load and speed.

Takeaway: Shaft alignment addresses axis geometry; soft foot addresses mounting support. Both can affect vibration, but neither can be diagnosed confidently from a single number. Source documentation, safe isolation and repeatable measurement are central to competent troubleshooting.
