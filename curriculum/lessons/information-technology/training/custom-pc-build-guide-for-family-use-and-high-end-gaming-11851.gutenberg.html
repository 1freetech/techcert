When building a custom PC, the first step is understanding the user’s specific requirements.

A basic family desktop is typically used for web browsing, document creation, streaming media, light photo editing, and running basic productivity applications.

For such a system, a budget-friendly quad-core processor (such as an Intel Core i3 or AMD Ryzen 3) will offer enough performance for everyday tasks.

8GB of DDR4 RAM is considered the minimum for a smooth experience with modern operating systems and applications.

For storage, a 256GB or 512GB SSD is preferred over a traditional hard disk drive for faster boot times and application loading, with the option to add a secondary HDD for bulk storage if needed.

The motherboard should have integrated graphics (found in most budget CPUs) to avoid the extra cost of a dedicated GPU. Additional essential components include a reliable 400W–500W power supply unit (PSU), a mid-tower case with adequate airflow, and basic peripherals such as a keyboard, mouse, and 1080p monitor.

In contrast, a high-end gaming desktop demands significantly more powerful components to meet the performance requirements of modern games at high resolutions and frame rates.

The system should include a high-performance multi-core processor, such as an Intel Core i7/i9 or AMD Ryzen 7/9. 16GB to 32GB of DDR4 or DDR5 RAM is recommended for multitasking and smooth gameplay. The centerpiece of a gaming build is the dedicated graphics card (GPU), where choices such as the NVIDIA RTX 4070/4080 or AMD Radeon RX 7900 series offer excellent performance for AAA gaming titles.

For storage, a 1TB NVMe SSD ensures fast load times, and additional HDD or SSD space can be added for game libraries and media. A 750W to 850W PSU from a reputable brand is recommended, especially when powering a high-end GPU and CPU. Gaming desktops also often include features like RGB lighting, high-airflow or liquid cooling, and a gaming monitor supporting higher refresh rates (144Hz or 240Hz) with technologies such as G-Sync or FreeSync.

Both builds require consideration of compatibility between components, including CPU socket types, RAM speed support, PSU wattage, and case dimensions.

It is crucial to check that the chosen motherboard supports the required ports (USB 3.x, HDMI, DisplayPort, Ethernet), expansion slots, and RAM configurations.

For the certification exam and real-world scenarios, selecting the correct components is not just about matching performance needs but also considering budget, upgradeability, and power efficiency. Documentation, such as system diagrams or customer requirement sheets, is often used to communicate and confirm component selections with clients before purchase.
