<!-- wp:paragraph --><p><strong>Paper money changed the meaning of money because the value of the payment instrument no longer had to come mainly from the material in the object itself.</strong> A metal coin could carry value through its metal content, official stamp, or both. A paper note was different: the paper itself was cheap. Its usefulness depended on a network of <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/05/oshistory-003-standardized-coinage-weights-mints-seigniorage-trust/"><strong>trust</strong></a>, redemption rules, taxation, government authority, merchant acceptance, and confidence that the issuer would not create more claims than the system could support.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>This lesson follows <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/14/oshistory-011-roman-coin-debasement-third-century-crisis/"><strong>OSHistory.011: Roman Coin Debasement and the Third-Century Crisis</strong></a>. Rome demonstrated that a state could alter the metallic content of <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/05/oshistory-003-standardized-coinage-weights-mints-seigniorage-trust/"><strong>coinage</strong></a>. Medieval China pushed the monetary experiment much further: merchants and governments learned that a transferable paper claim could circulate in place of heavy metal money.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Elementary Overview</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Imagine paying for a large shipment using thousands of metal coins. The money is durable, but it is heavy, bulky, expensive to transport, and vulnerable to theft. In parts of China, especially Sichuan, these problems became severe because commerce depended heavily on iron coin. Merchants began using transferable paper claims instead of physically moving every coin involved in every transaction.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>That innovation did not happen in one step. China moved through several stages: deposit and transfer instruments, privately issued notes, government regulation, state-issued paper currency, broader monetary circulation, and eventually episodes of depreciation when issuance outran the system’s ability to preserve confidence and purchasing power.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Learning Objectives</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul class="wp-block-list"><li>Explain why heavy metal coinage created a demand for lighter payment instruments.</li><li>Distinguish Tang-era <strong>flying money</strong> from later circulating paper currency.</li><li>Explain how private merchant notes in Sichuan developed into <strong>jiaozi</strong>.</li><li>Describe why the Song state took control of paper-note issuance.</li><li>Explain how taxation, redemption, reserves, legal rules, and acceptance supported note value.</li><li>Connect woodblock printing and administrative technology to monetary scaling.</li><li>Explain why counterfeiting became a new technical and legal problem.</li><li>Distinguish representative paper claims from increasingly fiat-like money.</li><li>Explain how excessive issuance can create depreciation and <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/12/oshistory-009-inflation-money-supply-purchasing-power/"><strong>inflation</strong></a>.</li><li>Relate Chinese paper-money experiments to later banknotes, ledger money, and digital money.</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Before Paper Money: The Weight Problem</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Metallic money solved important problems described earlier in <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/04/oshistory-002-commodity-money-why-some-goods-work-better-as-money/"><strong>commodity money</strong></a> and standardized coinage. Coins were recognizable, countable, divisible, and durable. But their physical nature created another constraint: large payments required large amounts of metal.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>In commercial regions where low-value copper or iron coins dominated, the logistics could become extreme. The <a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/rise-and-demise-of-paper-money-in-imperial-china/northern-song-monetary-policy-and-the-invention-of-paper-currency-in-sichuan/8D4E47993802972BFFEE47EC0DF7CD22"><strong>Cambridge history of paper money in imperial China</strong></a> explains that Sichuan relied heavily on cumbersome iron coin. A trader moving substantial purchasing power therefore faced weight, transport, security, counting, and storage costs before a sale even occurred.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>This is a recurring monetary pattern: when the settlement asset is expensive to move, people search for a claim on that asset that can move more easily. The claim becomes economically important because it reduces transaction friction.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Tang Flying Money Was a Bridge, Not Yet Full Paper Currency</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>During the Tang dynasty, merchants used instruments commonly translated as <strong>flying money</strong>. A merchant could deposit coin with a trusted office or institution and receive a paper claim that could be redeemed elsewhere. The <a href="https://www.money.org/money-museum-virtual-exhibits-silk-case4/"><strong>American Numismatic Association</strong></a> describes these instruments as an important predecessor to paper currency but notes that they were not yet ordinary circulating money in the later jiaozi sense.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The distinction matters. A remittance receipt can transfer value without becoming a general <strong>medium of exchange</strong>. True circulating paper money emerges when third parties begin accepting the paper itself in payment because they expect others to accept or redeem it later.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>This mirrors the logic of <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/05/oshistory-004-early-banking-ledger-money-deposits-banknotes-checks-reserves/"><strong>ledger money and early banking</strong></a>: a claim on stored value can become increasingly money-like as transferability and confidence expand.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Sichuan Merchants Created Jiaozi</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>In the late tenth century, private merchants in Sichuan began issuing paper bills known as <strong>jiaozi</strong>. According to Cambridge University Press, these notes emerged in the 990s and became useful for regional exchange because they were much easier to transport than iron coin.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The innovation was partly technological and partly institutional. Paper already existed. Printing already existed. Accounting already existed. What changed was the combination: merchants created standardized transferable claims that other people were willing to accept because the issuing firms were expected to honor them.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>That means the invention of paper money was not simply the invention of a new physical object. It was the invention of a monetary <strong>system of promises</strong>.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Private Issuers Introduced a New Kind of Risk</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Once private notes circulated, users had to evaluate the issuer. Could the merchant redeem the note? Did the issuer hold enough assets? Was the note authentic? Would another merchant accept it? Could the issuer disappear or fail?</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Cambridge’s history reports that private issuance eventually ran into solvency problems. A paper claim is convenient precisely because it separates payment from the physical reserve, but that separation creates the possibility that outstanding claims can exceed immediately available reserves. Confidence can disappear quickly when holders begin to doubt redemption.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>This is conceptually related to the <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/06/oshistory-005-bank-runs-rise-central-banking-panics-clearinghouses-lender-last-resort-elastic-currency/"><strong>bank-run problem</strong></a>: a financial claim can function smoothly for years and still become unstable when many holders demand settlement at once.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Song Government Took Over Issuance</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>After attempts to regulate the private system, the Song government took direct control of jiaozi issuance in the early eleventh century. Cambridge dates the state takeover to 1024, while some institutional summaries describe the transition beginning in 1023. The important point is not the one-year difference in administrative dating; it is the institutional shift from privately issued merchant notes to government-controlled paper currency.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Once the state became issuer, the note gained support from more than merchant reputation. Government procurement, taxes, public payments, redemption policies, issue limits, and state authority could all contribute to acceptance. Paper currency became part of the fiscal system rather than merely a private convenience.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Printing Technology Became Monetary Infrastructure</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Paper currency requires reproducibility, but it also requires controlled reproducibility. If anyone can make an indistinguishable note, the monetary system fails. China’s advanced <strong>papermaking</strong>, <strong>woodblock printing</strong>, ink production, engraving, seals, and bureaucracy made large-scale note issuance possible while also creating techniques for making official notes difficult to copy.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The note therefore became a technological object. Its value depended not only on monetary policy but also on printing quality, distinctive designs, official seals, recordkeeping, controlled plates, enforcement, and counterfeit detection.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Modern banknotes still solve the same problem with different technology: specialized substrates, serial numbers, microprinting, holographic elements, ultraviolet features, color-shifting inks, embedded threads, machine-readable structures, and centralized issuance all extend the same basic principle—make authentic money easy enough to verify and difficult enough to reproduce illegally.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">A Paper Note Is a Claim Before It Is a Commodity</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Gold, silver, copper, iron, cattle, salt, and other forms of <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/04/oshistory-002-commodity-money-why-some-goods-work-better-as-money/"><strong>commodity money</strong></a> have nonmonetary properties or production costs that help limit supply. Paper has very little intrinsic value relative to a large denomination printed on it.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The value of a paper note therefore comes mainly from the monetary arrangement around it: what it can be redeemed for, what debts or taxes it can settle, who accepts it, how scarce the issue is, and whether the issuer remains credible.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>This is one reason the history of paper money is so important. It makes monetary trust visible. The user is not primarily valuing the paper. The user is valuing the <strong>promise and network represented by the paper</strong>.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Tax Acceptance Can Create Powerful Demand</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>If a government accepts its own notes for taxes or uses them for official expenditures, the notes gain a built-in use case. People need the notes because the state itself accepts or requires them within parts of the fiscal system. Cambridge describes this relationship as <strong>fiscal circulation</strong>: taxation, state procurement, government expenditure, and interregional transfers helped paper money circulate as part of Song political economy.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>This does not mean taxation alone determines money’s value. Market acceptance, scarcity, convertibility, confidence, trade conditions, military events, and supply policy all matter. But state acceptance can create an important baseline demand for a currency.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Paper Money Made Supply More Elastic</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Mining and minting metal takes time and resources. Printing paper is much easier to scale. That makes paper currency administratively flexible, but it also changes the constraint on money creation.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Under metal coinage, a government might debase coins by reducing precious-metal content, as examined in OSHistory.011. Under paper currency, the state can expand nominal claims directly by issuing more notes. The technological cost of creating another note is tiny compared with the purchasing power printed on it.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The monetary constraint therefore shifts away from the physical production cost of the medium and toward rules, reserves, redemption, institutional credibility, taxation capacity, and public confidence.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">The First Paper-Money Inflation Problem</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The <a href="https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/docs/publications/frbatlreview/rev_frbatl_199311.pdf"><strong>Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta</strong></a> has described twelfth-century China as an early recorded paper-currency inflation. The basic mechanism is familiar: when the quantity of notes expands faster than the credible backing, redemption capacity, or demand for the currency, holders begin discounting the notes.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>This links directly to <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/12/oshistory-009-inflation-money-supply-purchasing-power/"><strong>money supply and purchasing power</strong></a>. Inflation is not simply “printing money” as a slogan. Prices reflect interactions among money supply, money demand, production, fiscal conditions, credit, expectations, velocity, and institutional credibility. But a rapidly expanding paper issue can remove one of the natural supply constraints that metal money imposed.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Paper money therefore created both a major efficiency gain and a major governance challenge: how can a society obtain the convenience of elastic money without destroying trust through excessive issuance?</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Song Paper Money Was Not One Uniform National Currency</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>It is easy to simplify the history by saying “China invented paper money,” but the actual system was regional and evolved over time. Cambridge’s current research emphasizes that early jiaozi was centered in Sichuan. Later Song governments authorized other paper currencies, including <strong>huizi</strong>, and different monetary regions could operate under different rules.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>That matters because monetary systems rarely appear fully formed. Technologies spread through trial, regional experimentation, institutional learning, political crisis, and administrative adaptation.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Yuan Dynasty Expanded the Experiment</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Under Mongol rule, especially during the era of Kublai Khan, paper currency became even more prominent across a much larger imperial territory. European travelers such as Marco Polo famously described the use of paper money in China with amazement because the monetary system looked radically different from the metal-heavy systems familiar to Europeans.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The <a href="https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/a-brief-summary-coins-currency-8947/fulltext"><strong>Federal Reserve historical collection</strong></a> preserves older educational accounts describing Marco Polo’s report of official paper notes under Kublai Khan. Modern scholarship adds more nuance: convertibility, regional use, reserves, state finance, and later depreciation changed over time rather than following one simple model.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why Trust Matters More With Paper</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>A coin can be weighed, assayed, or inspected for metal content. A paper note cannot independently prove the solvency or restraint of its issuer. That means paper monetary systems depend heavily on institutional credibility.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul class="wp-block-list"><li><strong>Authenticity:</strong> Is the note genuine?</li><li><strong>Scarcity:</strong> How many notes are being issued?</li><li><strong>Redemption:</strong> Can the note be exchanged for another settlement asset when promised?</li><li><strong>Acceptance:</strong> Will merchants, tax offices, and other users take it?</li><li><strong>Rule stability:</strong> Will the issuer suddenly change terms?</li><li><strong>Issuer solvency:</strong> Can promised obligations actually be met?</li><li><strong>Political stability:</strong> Will the authority supporting the note still exist tomorrow?</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>These questions did not disappear when modern central banks emerged. They became central features of monetary policy, banking, payment systems, and sovereign finance.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Representative Money and Fiat Money</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Not every paper note is the same type of money. A <strong>representative note</strong> is a claim redeemable for something else, such as metal or coin. A more <strong>fiat-like note</strong> derives its monetary role primarily from issuer authority, legal structure, tax acceptance, scarcity policy, and social acceptance rather than a fixed promise to exchange it for a commodity.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>China experimented with multiple points along that spectrum centuries before the modern world abandoned formal gold convertibility. Later lessons such as <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/08/oshistory-006-gold-standard-convertibility-parity-reserves-redemption/"><strong>the gold standard</strong></a>, <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/10/oshistory-007-bretton-woods-dollar-gold-system-imf-world-bank-road-to-1971/"><strong>Bretton Woods</strong></a>, and <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2026/10/11/oshistory-008-nixon-shock-end-dollar-gold-convertibility/"><strong>the Nixon Shock</strong></a> show how the redemption question remained central for nearly another millennium.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Paper Money Was Also an Information Technology</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Money carries information: denomination, issuer, authenticity, ownership or possession, settlement rules, and purchasing power. A paper note compressed a large amount of economic information into a portable physical token.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>That is why the history of money overlaps with the history of technology. The evolution from coin to paper to bank ledger to card network to digital wallet to <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2023/11/04/bitcoin-and-the-history-of-the-u-s-dollar/"><strong>Bitcoin</strong></a> is partly a history of how societies record, verify, transfer, and settle claims.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The medium changes, but the engineering questions remain recognizable: Who can issue? Who can verify? How is double spending prevented? What limits supply? What happens when records disagree? What is the final settlement asset? Who can reverse a payment? What happens if the issuer fails?</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Paper Money Solved</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul class="wp-block-list"><li>Reduced the physical burden of transporting large quantities of coin.</li><li>Made large commercial payments easier to move across distance.</li><li>Supported expanding trade and state fiscal operations.</li><li>Allowed denominations to be represented without equivalent-weight metal.</li><li>Made monetary supply more responsive to administrative decisions.</li><li>Enabled sophisticated printing and verification systems to become part of money.</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Paper Money Did Not Solve</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list --><ul class="wp-block-list"><li>Counterfeiting.</li><li>Issuer insolvency.</li><li>Overissuance.</li><li>Political misuse of monetary issuance.</li><li>Loss of confidence.</li><li>Inflation and depreciation.</li><li>The need for credible settlement rules.</li><li>The need for reliable records and administration.</li></ul><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} --><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Answers</h3><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} --><ol class="wp-block-list"><li>Large payments required heavy quantities of metal, creating transport, counting, storage, security, and transaction costs.</li><li>Flying money functioned mainly as a remittance or transferable claim for deposited funds rather than a broadly circulating general-purpose currency.</li><li>Jiaozi were paper bills that emerged among merchants in Sichuan and later became state-controlled paper currency.</li><li>The value of the note depended on the issuer remaining able and willing to redeem or honor outstanding claims.</li><li>Paper currency became integrated with taxation, public expenditure, regulation, issue policy, and government authority.</li><li>Mass issuance required controlled paper, engraving, printing, seals, recordkeeping, authentication, and anti-counterfeiting methods.</li><li>The physical paper has little value compared with its denomination, so users depend on the monetary system and issuer behind it.</li><li>If a government accepts or requires its currency for taxes, people have a recurring reason to obtain and hold that currency.</li><li>Paper notes are cheaper and faster to create than mining, refining, and minting equivalent purchasing power in metal.</li><li>Representative money promises redemption into another asset; fiat-like money relies more heavily on institutional authority, acceptance, scarcity rules, and monetary credibility.</li><li>If note supply grows faster than demand, credible reserves, or productive capacity, users may discount the notes and prices measured in those notes can rise.</li><li>A note is a portable information system representing issuer, denomination, authenticity, settlement rules, and transferable value.</li></ol><!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Elementary Conclusion</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The rise of paper money in China was not simply a story about replacing coins with paper. It was a shift from moving physical value to moving <strong>claims on value</strong>. That shift made commerce lighter, faster, and more scalable, but it also made trust, accounting, authentication, monetary policy, and state credibility more important than ever.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>The fundamental lesson is simple: <strong>paper money works because people trust the system around the paper.</strong> Once value can be represented by information rather than by the physical substance of the payment object, money becomes easier to move—and easier to create. Every later monetary system has had to manage that tradeoff.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
