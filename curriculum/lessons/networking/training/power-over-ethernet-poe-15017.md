<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Power over Ethernet (PoE) is a networking standard that delivers <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2025/04/09/power-supply-types-ratings-and-features/">DC electrical power</a> along with data signals through standard twisted-pair <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2025/04/18/rj11-vs-rj45/">Ethernet cabling</a> (typically <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2025/04/09/cat5e-vs-cat6-ethernet-cables/">Cat5e</a>, <a href="https://bitcoinversus.tech/2025/04/09/cat5e-vs-cat6-ethernet-cables/">Cat6</a>, or higher). </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This innovation eliminates the need for separate power cords or outlets near devices, simplifying installation and reducing infrastructure costs.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<p>PoE works by injecting power into the Ethernet cable from a Power Sourcing Equipment (PSE)—such as a PoE switch or injector—and delivering it to a Powered Device (PD) like an IP camera, wireless access point, VoIP phone, or IoT sensor. The technology is governed by IEEE standards:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>IEEE 802.3af</strong>: Up to 15.4W per port</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>IEEE 802.3at (PoE+)</strong>: Up to 30W per port</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>IEEE 802.3bt (PoE++ or 4PPoE)</strong>: Up to 60W or 90W per port</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>PoE uses either spare wire pairs or data pairs in the Ethernet cable to transmit power, depending on the standard and device compatibility. It supports data rates of 10/100/1000 Mbps and can power devices up to 100 meters away.</p>
